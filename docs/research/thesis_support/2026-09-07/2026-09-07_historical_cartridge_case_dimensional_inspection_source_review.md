# Historical Cartridge-Case Dimensional Inspection — Source Review

**Project:** CaseMetrology
**Date:** 2026-09-07
**Purpose:** Chapter 2 literature-review evidence record
**Status:** Source-based working review

## 1. Purpose

This review examines historical cartridge-case inspection literature that is
directly relevant to the CaseMetrology thesis.

The purpose is to establish what had already been demonstrated in optical and
electro-optical cartridge-case dimensional inspection before CaseMetrology and
to prevent unsupported novelty claims.

These sources are used as historical technical literature. Claims below are
restricted to what the reviewed source documents support.

---

## 2. High Throughput Dimensional Measurements with Electro-Optics

### Source

K. L. Swinth, R. A. Walker, and N. G. Nedrow,
"High throughput dimensional measurements with electro-optics,"
Pacific Northwest Laboratory.

### System purpose

The paper describes a profile monitor developed as part of an advanced
automatic high-speed cartridge-case inspection system for 5.56 mm cartridge
cases.

The complete cartridge case measurement/eject system included:

- a mechanical handling system;
- electro-optical dimensional measurement instrumentation;
- system-monitoring instrumentation;
- processing electronics; and
- a dedicated computer.

The system was intended for manufacturing-line inspection and automated
rejection of unacceptable cases.

### Measured dimensions

The profile monitor measured five cartridge-case dimensions:

1. head thickness;
2. head diameter;
3. extractor-groove diameter;
4. gas-seal length; and
5. total length.

The source therefore establishes clear historical precedent for optical or
electro-optical measurement of cartridge-case total length.

### Optical measurement architecture

The profile monitor used:

- a 450-W xenon arc lamp;
- collimated transmitted illumination;
- an imaging lens;
- projected shadows of the case and gauging fixture;
- fiber-optic image-folding light guides;
- linear CCD sensing; and
- electronic shadow-edge detection.

The linear sensor contained 256 elements over approximately 0.125 in, giving a
reported element spacing of approximately 0.0005 in.

The measurement principle therefore relied substantially on optical silhouette
or shadow-edge detection.

### Mechanical-reference distinction

The historical system did not measure total length solely as the separation
between two freely presented cartridge-case silhouette endpoints.

The cartridge case was mechanically held in a gauging fixture. Length and
thickness measurements used precision fixture surfaces as references.

For total length, the measurement was made between an upper spindle reference
and a spring-loaded tip contacting the case mouth.

This distinction is important for CaseMetrology. The historical system provides
prior art for electro-optical cartridge-case total-length measurement, but its
mechanically registered measurement architecture differs materially from the
independent free-placement condition used in formal CaseMetrology V1
acquisition.

### Reported performance

The paper reports dimensional measurements at a throughput rate of approximately
1200 cases per minute and reports measurement standard deviation on the order of
0.0001 in under the described measurement conditions.

The source also describes measurements on standards and comparison of profile-
monitor measurements with hand-gauged measurements on manufactured cartridge
cases.

Known standards could be inserted into the operating system, and selected
production specimens could be retrieved for comparison with conventional
gauging.

### Relevance to CaseMetrology

This paper is central historical prior art because it establishes that:

- optical cartridge-case dimensional inspection predates CaseMetrology;
- total cartridge-case length had previously been measured electro-optically;
- backlighting and shadow-edge detection had already been used;
- dimensional measurements had been integrated with automatic accept/reject
  decisions;
- reference standards and hand-gauge comparisons had been used for measurement
  assurance; and
- high-throughput manufacturing inspection had already been demonstrated.

Accordingly, CaseMetrology shall not be described as the first optical,
computer-assisted, automated, or manufacturing-oriented cartridge-case OAL
measurement system.

## 3. Automatic Cartridge Case Inspection and Process Control Monitor

### Source

W. J. Coleman and K. L. Swinth,
"Automatic Cartridge Case Inspection and Process Control Monitor,"
Battelle, Pacific Northwest Laboratories.

### System purpose

This source describes an advanced cartridge case measurement/eject system
designed to inspect and reject manufactured cases automatically at rates up to
approximately 1200 cases per minute.

The system combined:

- mechanical case handling;
- dimensional measurement;
- surface-flaw inspection;
- vent-hole inspection;
- split/fold inspection;
- computer control;
- automated rejection; and
- process-data reporting.

### Dimensional measurement

Five cartridge-case dimensions were measured using an electro-optical system
and diode-array sensing:

- head thickness;
- head diameter;
- extractor-groove diameter;
- gas-seal length; and
- total length.

The paper reports measurement standard deviations as small as approximately
2.5 micrometers under the described conditions.

### Comparison with conventional gauging

The paper states that measurements obtained by the automatic inspection system
fell within the uncertainties of hand-gauged values for the same cartridge
cases.

This is directly relevant to CaseMetrology because it demonstrates historical
use of an external conventional measurement process to assess optical
dimensional measurements.

The exact statistical framework should not be represented as equivalent to the
CaseMetrology specimen-level agreement study unless the source explicitly
supports that interpretation.

### Calibration and system assurance

The paper emphasizes that calibration and continuous monitoring of an automatic
inspection system are essential.

The advanced system automated collection and processing of calibration and
quality-assurance data.

The mechanical system allowed calibrated or known samples to be inserted and
retrieved so that the measurement system could be checked during operation.

The authors explicitly treated automatic inspection equipment as requiring
continuous verification rather than assuming that correct operation could be
taken for granted.

### Relevance to CaseMetrology

This source provides historical precedent for several ideas relevant to the
CaseMetrology research framework:

- optical dimensional cartridge-case measurement;
- comparison with hand-gauged reference values;
- use of known or calibrated check artifacts;
- automated calibration / QA evaluation;
- explicit verification of measurement-system operation;
- manufacturing process monitoring; and
- automatic classification and rejection.

CaseMetrology's use of reference checks and a calibrated comparator therefore
should not be described as an unprecedented concept. Its research contribution
must instead be evaluated according to the particular architecture,
measurement model, experimental design, validation criteria, and evidence
governance used in the present thesis.

## 4. On-Line Automatic High Speed Inspection of Cartridge Cases

### Source

W. J. Coleman, F. R. Reich, M. D. Erickson, and W. S. Kelly,
"On-Line Automatic High Speed Inspection of Cartridge Cases,"
Battelle, Pacific Northwest Laboratory.

### System purpose

This earlier system was developed as an automatic cartridge-case inspection
and reject system.

It combined mechanical handling, electro-optical inspection, eddy-current
inspection, computer control, data acquisition, and automatic rejection.

The manufacturing system operated at a nominal rate of approximately 1200
cases per minute.

### Profile measurement

The cartridge-case profile system used linear diode arrays for electro-optical
measurement.

The source describes discrete diameter measurements at multiple prescribed
positions and reports output of:

- multiple diameter measurements;
- total length;
- head thickness; and
- gas-seal length.

A stated dimensional design goal was approximately ±0.001 in at each measured
position.

### Calibration and verification

The mechanical inspection system provided means for inserting calibration
standards to verify measurement operation.

This shows that measurement-assurance concepts were present in the earlier
development of cartridge-case electro-optical inspection and were not limited
to later system versions.

### Relevance to CaseMetrology

This source establishes an earlier stage in the development of automated
electro-optical cartridge-case inspection.

It provides historical evidence of:

- optical profile gauging;
- cartridge-case total-length measurement;
- multiple dimensional measurements;
- calibration-standard insertion;
- computer-controlled data acquisition;
- high-throughput production inspection; and
- automated rejection.

## 5. Cross-Source Synthesis

Taken together, the three reviewed historical sources establish that
electro-optical cartridge-case dimensional inspection was technically mature
enough decades ago to support:

- total-length measurement;
- multiple diameter and length measurements;
- transmitted-light or shadow-based optical sensing;
- mechanically controlled specimen presentation;
- computer processing;
- predefined dimensional limits;
- automatic accept/reject decisions;
- high-throughput manufacturing operation;
- insertion of known or calibrated standards;
- comparison with hand-gauged measurements; and
- process-level statistical reporting.

This historical evidence substantially constrains any CaseMetrology novelty
claim.

The thesis shall not claim that CaseMetrology invented:

- optical cartridge-case inspection;
- electro-optical cartridge-case dimensional measurement;
- optical measurement of cartridge-case total length;
- computer-assisted cartridge-case dimensional inspection;
- automatic cartridge-case dimensional classification; or
- the use of measurement standards to verify an optical cartridge-case
  inspection system.

## 6. Technically Relevant Differences from CaseMetrology

The historical systems and CaseMetrology address related dimensional inspection
problems but use materially different architectures.

The reviewed historical profile-monitor systems relied on dedicated mechanical
handling and gauging fixtures, optical projection, fiber-optic image transfer,
and linear diode or CCD arrays.

CaseMetrology V1 instead uses:

- a modern two-dimensional area camera;
- embedded computing;
- a software-defined segmentation and OAL estimator;
- spatial and directional pixel-to-physical calibration;
- preserved raw image acquisition;
- a static backlit measurement surface;
- independent free specimen placement during formal V1 CV acquisition;
- prospective method freeze;
- a separately calibrated manual reference process;
- repeated measurements under independently randomized run orders;
- prospectively frozen statistical acceptance criteria; and
- version-controlled preservation of software, data, analysis, and research
  provenance.

These differences describe the research space but do not, by themselves,
establish novelty.

Novelty or contribution language must be based on the complete Chapter 2
literature review rather than architectural difference alone.

## 7. Implication for the Thesis Contribution

The defensible thesis contribution should not rest on being the first system to
measure cartridge-case OAL optically.

The literature review should instead determine whether the meaningful
contribution lies in the integrated combination of:

- embedded area-camera metrology;
- static backlit cartridge-case OAL measurement;
- software-defined dimensional estimation;
- prospective configuration and method freeze;
- spatial/directional calibration;
- independent calibrated manual-reference comparison;
- formal repeated-placement characterization;
- stratified prospective validation;
- predefined engineering acceptance criteria;
- explicit measurement-failure accounting;
- reference-uncertainty treatment; and
- preservation of raw data, software state, analysis artifacts, and Git
  provenance.

No individual attribute or combination of attributes shall be called novel
until the remaining literature search has been completed.

These historical sources belong primarily in Section 2.6, Historical
Cartridge-Case Dimensional Inspection.

They should also be referenced in the final Chapter 2 synthesis because they
